## I. THE HUMAN ALGORITHM

<!-- DESIGN NOTE: Open on black. White text. No navigation visible until scroll. -->
<!-- PHOTO: Jenson. Candid. Not posed. The reader meets him before they meet the idea. -->

There is an algorithm no one talks about in boardrooms.

It does not optimize. It does not scale. It cannot be fine-tuned, benchmarked,
or deployed. It is the set of assumptions — quiet, inherited, mostly unexamined —
that determines who technology is built for, and who it leaves behind.

I spent fifteen years in the technology industry before I understood this.
My son taught me in an afternoon.

---

Jenson was born into a world that was not designed for him. The interfaces
were wrong. The inputs assumed a user he would never be. The systems I had
spent my career building — elegant, efficient, frictionless for millions —
had nothing to say to my son.

He could not tap, swipe, type, or speak the way the products expected.
And so the products ignored him.

This is not a metaphor. This is what happened.

---

> I was telling boardrooms to put humans first while the most important
> human in my life was showing me I didn't know what that meant.

---

I had built innovation strategies for banks on four continents. I had advised
executives at Microsoft, Google, and Visa on how to meet their customers
where they are. I had stood on stages and told audiences that technology
must serve people.

Jenson sat in my living room and made all of it theoretical.

The gap was not in my knowledge. It was in my premise. I had been optimizing
for the average user. Jenson is not the average user. He is the edge case
that reveals whether your system actually works — or whether it only works
for people who were already fine without it.

That distinction changes everything.

---

<!-- DESIGN NOTE: Whitespace break. Full viewport. Single line centered. -->

**Technology's job is not efficiency. It is inclusion.**
**Efficiency is a feature. Inclusion is the function.**

## II. THE FRONT LINES

<!-- PHOTO: Stage shot or editorial portrait. Professional context. The shift from personal to proof. -->

I did not arrive at this understanding from the outside.

Before Jenson, there were twenty years on the front lines of every major
shift in how people interact with money, information, and each other.

At **WING Money**, an ANZ subsidiary in Cambodia, I saw what happens when
you build financial infrastructure for a population that has never had a
bank account. The design constraints were absolute: low literacy, shared
devices, intermittent connectivity. You could not default to the assumptions
that worked in Sydney or Singapore. You had to start from the human and
work backward to the technology.

At **Visa**, through Fundamo, I worked at the intersection of global payments
infrastructure and emerging markets — where a mobile phone is not a convenience
but the entire financial system. The question was never "how do we digitize
what exists?" It was "what do we build for people the existing system
never considered?"

At **Moven**, I helped build the world's first downloadable bank. No branches.
No paperwork. No legacy. A financial institution designed from a blank screen
for a user who had never known anything else. We did not digitize banking.
We asked what banking would look like if no one had invented it yet, and
built that instead.

---

> Three jobs. One question each time:
> Who is this not working for — and what would it take to include them?

---

Two books came out of those years. *Mobile Ready* documented the behavioral
shift of six billion people carrying connected devices — the untethered
consumer who would not wait for institutions to catch up. *Innovation Wars*
gave corporate leaders the frameworks to stop defending old models and start
building new ones.

The thesis across both was the same: the organizations that survive are the
ones that design for the person they have not met yet.

I did not know, when I wrote that, that the person I had not met yet
was already in my house.

## III. ISAAC

<!-- DESIGN NOTE: New section. Shift in rhythm. Shorter paragraphs. Present tense. -->
<!-- No photo here. Isaac is an idea. He does not need a face. -->

Isaac is nineteen.

He has never walked into a bank. He has never filled out a form without
asking why. He has never accepted a product that asked him to adapt to it
rather than the other way around.

He is not hypothetical. He is the demographic reality sitting in your
customer data, your employee pipeline, and your competitor's founding team.

---

Isaac grew up in Jenson's world.

Not literally — Isaac does not have Jenson's challenges. But he grew up in
a culture that Jenson's generation forced into existence: one where
accessibility is not a compliance checkbox but a baseline expectation. Where
transparency is not a brand value but a filtering mechanism. Where inclusion
is not a program — it is the minimum viable product.

Jenson required technology to include him. Isaac *expects* it.

That is the shift.

---

> Jenson taught me who technology forgets.
> Isaac is what happens when that generation refuses to be forgotten.

---

The organizations I work with — the ones running AI strategies, redesigning
customer journeys, rethinking workforce models — are preparing for a future
defined by compute power, large language models, and automation. They are
right to prepare.

But they are preparing for the technology. They are not preparing for Isaac.

Isaac does not care about your AI strategy. He cares whether your AI strategy
cares about him. He will not read your mission statement. He will test it —
silently, instantly, and without giving you a second chance.

He evaluates every interaction against four criteria, whether he knows it or not:

**Transparency.** Can I see how this decision was made?
**Autonomy.** Can I control what happens to my data, my attention, my time?
**Immediacy.** Does this meet me where I am, right now, on my terms?
**Inclusion.** Was this built for someone like me — or was I an afterthought?

Fail one, and Isaac leaves. He does not complain. He does not churn visibly.
He simply never arrives.

That was the shift I warned about. Then the shift got a successor.

## IV. ESTHER

<!-- DESIGN NOTE: The rhythm changes again. Shorter than Isaac. Colder. The pace of something arriving faster than you expected. -->
<!-- TYPOGRAPHIC SHIFT: If Isaac's section used the accent color sparingly, Esther's name appears in it every time. She is the new signal. -->

Esther was born on November 30, 2022.

So was ChatGPT.

---

She will not remember a world before generative AI. She will not distinguish
between "using a tool" and "having a conversation." She will not understand
why your onboarding flow requires twelve steps when her AI agent completed
it in one.

Isaac chose to leave. Esther will never arrive in the first place.

---

Isaac is the mobile native. The social native. The consumer who grew up
with a screen in his hand and a swipe as his default verb. He is the shift
that most organizations are still trying to absorb.

Esther is what comes after absorption.

She is the AI native. The agentic native. She does not interact with your
product. Her agent does. She does not compare your service to a competitor.
Her model does — instantly, across every alternative, with no loyalty
and no memory of your brand promise.

---

> Isaac disrupted your customer journey.
> Esther dissolved it.

---

Here is the generational pincer that no strategy deck accounts for:

Isaac is still in the room. He is your customer today, your mid-career
employee, your most vocal critic on the platforms you still don't fully
understand. He is not going away. He is getting more demanding.

And behind him — three years old right now, learning language alongside
a machine that learns it too — Esther is forming expectations that make
Isaac's look quaint.

Isaac expects inclusion. Esther expects intelligence.
Isaac expects transparency. Esther expects autonomy *delegated to an agent
she trusts more than your interface.*
Isaac expects immediacy. Esther expects anticipation — the answer before
the question, the solution before the friction.

And inclusion? For Esther, inclusion is not a design principle.
It is a fundamental right. Baked in. Non-negotiable. If Jenson cannot
use it, it does not exist in Esther's world. Not because she read a
policy. Because her AI will refuse to route her to a product that
excludes.

---

<!-- DESIGN NOTE: Full viewport. Two lines stacked. High contrast. -->

**Isaac defined the shift.**
**Esther is the outcome.**

---

The Human Algorithm I described at the beginning of this story — the
quiet set of assumptions that determines who technology serves — is being
rewritten. Not by executives. Not by engineers. By a generation that will
delegate the question entirely.

Esther will not ask "was this built for me?"

Her agent will ask it for her. And if the answer is no, Esther will never
know your company existed.

None of it would have happened the way it did without a boy who could not

Jenson did not make me a better speaker. He made me a more honest one.
He gave me a question I carried into every room for a decade. Isaac gave
that question urgency. Esther gave it a deadline.

## EDITORIAL NOTES

### Voice Calibration
- **First person throughout.** This is not a bio. It is a confession that becomes a thesis.
- **No adjectives selling Scott.** The reader should finish thinking "this person earned this perspective" — not "this person told me they're impressive."
- **Cinematic pacing.** One idea per scroll. The whitespace does the work that adjectives would ruin.
- **The Jenson sections are warm but not sentimental.** Fact, observation, implication. No "heartwarming journey" language.
- **The Isaac sections are cool and direct.** Present tense. Short sentences. Boardroom cadence.
- **The Esther sections are colder still.** Clipped. Inevitable. The tone of something already in motion. If Isaac reads like a warning, Esther reads like a weather report — it is not asking your permission.

### Narrative Architecture — The Three-Body Arc
The story now moves through three named personas, each representing a layer of the Human Algorithm:

| Persona  | Born       | Archetype        | Relationship to Technology     | Role in Arc       |
|----------|-----------|------------------|-------------------------------|-------------------|
| Jenson   | —         | The edge case    | Technology excluded him        | The origin        |
| Isaac    | ~2007     | Mobile/Social native | Technology must include him | The shift         |
| Esther   | Nov 30, 2022 | AI/Agentic native | Technology is her native language | The outcome |

The pincer structure: organizations are caught between Isaac (who is already here, already demanding) and Esther (who is coming, and whose expectations will be set not by human experience but by AI baselines). Jenson is the moral foundation that gives the pincer its weight.

1. **Jenson specifics.** The opening section is deliberately restrained — it names the gap ("the interfaces were wrong") without inventing details about Jenson's condition or daily experience. 3. **Isaac's age and details.** "Isaac is nineteen" is a placeholder archetype. Scott should confirm whether Isaac is a named persona from his existing talks with established characteristics, or whether this draft should define him fresh.
4. **The four criteria (Transparency, Autonomy, Immediacy, Inclusion).** These are drafted as the "Isaac Readiness Model" from the sitemap. Scott should confirm these are the right four, or replace with his actual framework if one exists.
5. **Esther and the blog post.** Scott's blog includes "From Digital Natives to AI Natives: Are You Ready for Esther?" — confirm that Esther is an established persona from that post and whether her characteristics (born on ChatGPT launch day, agentic-first, AI as fundamental right) align with how he has already presented her.
